\begin{abstract}
Governments, standards bodies and institutes have published many documents that mention testing AI systems before deployment, but they differ in force: some require testing, some recommend it and some only describe it. We build a clause level evidence table of {{n_rows}} provisions from {{n_docs_total}} public documents and analyse the {{n_test_head}} test provisions that pass a verification screen. Binding provisions (mandatory or conditional) are a minority, {{head_mc_pct}} of provisions and {{rb_doc_mean}}\% when every document is weighted equally, and stay below half under each coding assumption tried separately; combining the most favourable assumptions with resampling of documents does not rule out a majority ({{rb_fav_ge50}}\% of resamples reach half). Binding provisions are more often process only than the others, also within the same test type, and we find no evidence that they name methods or criteria more often. An audit of the table tempers these results: binding status is a property of the document and is modestly predictable from wording on unseen families; blind second codings agree with each other (kappa {{ag_k_blind}}, or {{mk_k_masked}} without the document name) and less with the original coding; a fresh sample found {{vf_err_s_pct}}\% strict errors in readable rows while {{vf_unread_pct}}\% could not be read; and an independent reference list suggests a recall of {{cp_rec}}\% on {{cp_pos}} confirmed candidates. All coders and verifiers are language models, and a human second coding and a legal review are not yet done.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Organisations that build or buy AI systems are told by many sources to test them before use. A procurement officer reading these sources meets three different things under one word. A statute may oblige a named actor to test. A national institute may recommend a testing practice. A standard may describe what testing typically involves. Treating all three as requirements overstates the obligations on an organisation, and treating all three as advice understates them. Which of the three applies depends on the document and on who the organisation is.

We present Ikhtibar (Arabic for test), a comparative evidence base on what published documents say about pre deployment testing of AI systems, coded by binding force and by type of test. It began as a gap analysis for public sector deployment in the United Arab Emirates and carries a section on what the retrieved UAE sources do and do not contain. A table of extracted provisions can look rigorous while resting on one analyst's coding and on rows never checked against the source, so the paper pairs the table with an audit of it. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the structure.

\paragraph{Research questions} We ask: \textbf{RQ1} How much of what documents say about pre deployment testing is binding, and does this depend on the family of documents or the type of test? \textbf{RQ2} Do binding provisions say how to test, more than other provisions do? \textbf{RQ3} Can the codes be predicted from simpler signals, and which signal drives them? \textbf{RQ4} Do independent coders reproduce the codes? \textbf{RQ5} Does the headline finding survive reasonable changes to coding choices? \textbf{RQ6} How often does a row misstate its source, and how much of the relevant literature does the table miss? \textbf{RQ7} Does a checklist tool built on the table behave correctly against a specification written before the tool was run?

\paragraph{Contributions} (1) A clause level evidence table with binding force, test type, source locator, retrieval date and verification status per row (Section~\ref{sec:method}). (2) A measured answer to RQ1 and RQ2, including a four level specificity coding of every headline row and a comparison that holds the test type fixed (Section~\ref{sec:findings}). (3) An audit protocol with document grouped baselines, blind second codings with and without the document name, robustness analysis, a fresh verification sample and a completeness pilot, with its results and its failures reported (Section~\ref{sec:audit}). (4) A secondary contribution, a three valued checklist tool and its evaluation against scenarios written from its specification (Section~\ref{sec:tool}). (5) A map from every number computed from the data to a committed result file (Appendix~\ref{app:trace}). This is research analysis and not legal advice.

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Comparative studies and datasets of AI governance} Comparisons of AI regulation across jurisdictions exist, for example of the European Union, the United States and China \citep{chun2024comparative,du2025toward}, a seven category framework applied to five jurisdictions \citep{roberts2023comparative}, and a comparison of risk classification and enforcement \citep{almaamari2025between}. The AI Governance and Regulatory Archive provides an annotated dataset of AI laws and policies, tagged at segment level with a taxonomy of risks, harms, governance strategies and application domains \citep{arnold2024agora}; we did not find binding force or test type among its tags. A computational study of 1,065 strategy and legislation documents measures hedging language across 64 countries \citep{silva2024decoding}, a document level use of wording cues that is related to force. Cross document comparisons of company frontier safety policies find nine shared components, among them capability thresholds and evaluation timing \citep{metr2025common}, and score twelve such frameworks against 65 criteria \citep{stelling2025evaluating}; a mapping of company practice to a draft code of practice is related \citep{stelling2025mapping}. These cover voluntary company commitments. We narrow the question to one activity, testing before deployment, and record the force of each provision at clause level across governments, institutes and standards bodies. We found no work that codes binding force of AI governance clauses, which is a limit of a search run under a restricted budget and not a claim that none exists.

\paragraph{Audit, evaluation and assurance} Internal auditing across the development lifecycle was proposed as a complement to external audits \citep{raji2020closing}; auditing at the level of providers, models and applications was proposed for language models \citep{mokander2023auditing}; black box access was argued to be insufficient \citep{casper2024blackbox}; third party audit ecosystems in finance, environment and health were compared \citep{raji2022outsider}; and a landscape analysis of 390 audit tools with interviews of 35 practitioners found thin support for harm discovery and audit communication \citep{ojewale2024towards}. Evaluation methods include holistic benchmarks \citep{liang2023holistic}, evaluations for dangerous capabilities \citep{shevlane2023model,phuong2024evaluating}, automated red teaming \citep{perez2022red} and a sociotechnical view of safety evaluation \citep{weidinger2023sociotechnical}; industry red teaming practices vary widely \citep{feffer2024red}, safety evaluations of generative systems leave gaps in coverage \citep{rauh2024gaps}, and benchmark quality is uneven \citep{reuel2024betterbench,bean2025measuring}. An evaluation science for generative systems is proposed \citep{weidinger2025toward}. These works describe how to test and how well tests are done. We ask where documents say that testing must, should or does occur, which matters because a clause that says ``test'' does not fix the quality of the test.

\paragraph{The European Union Act and standards} Conformity assessment and post market monitoring have been analysed \citep{mokander2021conformity}, harmonised standards examined \citep{gornet2024european}, and reliance on self certification criticised \citep{wachter2024limitations}; two analyses concern the 2021 proposal \citep{mokander2021conformity,veale2021demystifying}, while our table uses the amended text of 2026. The Act is described as a framework for regulatory learning whose compliance relies on CEN/CENELEC and ISO/IEC standards \citep{lewis2025mapping}, and attempts to translate its requirements into technical requirements find that some lack usable technical definitions \citep{guldimann2024complai}. Voluntary testing frameworks such as that of Singapore \citep{lee2023aiverify,harjani2025singapore} and the NIST framework \citep{tabassi2023airmf} are guidance that organisations may adopt, and NIST has outlined a standard for testing, evaluation, verification and validation \citep{nist2025tevv}.

\paragraph{The Gulf and the public sector} A document analysis of the six national AI strategies of the Gulf Cooperation Council finds reliance on soft regulation through strategies and ethical principles, with no comprehensive AI specific legislation at the time of its analysis \citep{albous2025gcc}; a legal overview describes non binding ethics guidelines and a non binding charter for the UAE \citep{hofverberg2024falqs}; a comparison of the UAE and Kuwait studies institutional mechanisms in public sector adoption and concludes that enforceable safeguards are needed, without comparing existing testing requirements \citep{albous2025kuwaituae}. Evaluation of Arabic language models is described as uneven in dialect coverage, with no standard hallucination benchmark \citep{alzubaidi2025evaluating}, and safety evaluation concentrates on a few languages \citep{peppin2025multilingual}. We found no study of testing requirements for AI in the UAE or the Gulf. For the public sector, impact assessment frameworks were proposed early \citep{reisman2018aia} and cautioned against convenient metrics \citep{metcalf2021aias}; a framework for supreme audit institutions covers the lifecycle \citep{brand2025sai}; a national audit office found that few of nine government algorithms met its requirements \citep{rekenkamer2022audit9}; fears of bureaucratic burden were reported for a transparency standard in policing \citep{zilka2023police}; and a government roadmap plans to strengthen the independent assurance market \citep{dsit2025assurance}. None compares pre deployment testing requirements across regulators.

\paragraph{Legal text classification and language models as coders} Classifying obligation, permission and prohibition in legal text is an established task \citep{oneill2017classifying,sleimi2020semantic,ariai2024legalsurvey}. On 200 provisions of European Union regulation annotated by three people with six deontic classes, a small model beat a fine tuned legal language model on macro F1 while rare classes went unrecognised \citep{minkova2023lowresource}; for sentences containing shall or must in about 120,000 European legal acts, two methods for deciding whether such a sentence regulates often disagreed \citep{brandsma2025regulatory}; and requirements engineering studies compare language models with simple baselines on regulatory provisions \citep{hassani2025foodsafety}. Hassani et al.~\citep{hassani2025foodsafety} use obligation, permission, power, prohibition, right and none; our binding status coding instead has five classes, including non binding recommendation and ambiguous force. Requirement levels such as MUST, SHOULD and MAY have formal definitions in some standards \citep{bradner1997key}, but conventions differ between document families, which motivates our wording rule baseline and its limits. Language models are increasingly used for annotation, with results from agreement above crowd workers \citep{gilardi2023chatgpt,tornberg2023chatgpt} to reliability that varies with prompt wording \citep{reiss2023testing}, performance comparable to well trained law student annotators on legal concepts when guidelines are supplied \citep{savelka2023can}, and a requirement of validation against human labels \citep{pangakis2023automated}; surveys and benchmarks cover the practice \citep{tan2024large,ziems2024can}. Models are also used to extract rules from the AI Act, with checking that is itself model based \citep{guliani2026dejure}, and to judge compliance with expert labels of only moderate agreement \citep{marino2025airegbench}. In evidence synthesis, a study within reviews found error rates of 9.0\% for model assisted and 11.0\% for human only data extraction \citep{gartlehner2025swar}, a joint position statement on model use in evidence synthesis asks for human oversight and reporting \citep{flemyng2025position}, and a reporting guideline covers studies that use language models \citep{gallifant2024tripodllm}. Because our coding was assisted by a language model, these results motivate blind second codings and a verification audit. Models fabricate references \citep{fabrication2023chatgpt,chelli2024hallucination,athaluri2023exploring,naser2026cite}, a risk for any table built with model assistance.

\paragraph{Agreement, completeness and preregistration} Agreement is reported with Cohen's kappa \citep{mchugh2012interrater} and Krippendorff's alpha \citep{krippendorff2011computing}; kappa is sensitive to prevalence and marginal imbalance, which an alternative coefficient is reported to handle better \citep{wongpakaran2013comparison} and sample size requirements for kappa tests depend on it too \citep{bujang2017guidelines}, so we report percentage agreement beside kappa. Capture recapture methods have been used to estimate what a literature search misses \citep{spoor1996use,kastner2009capture,stelfox2013capture}, with doubts about duplicate identification \citep{motschall2009number}, and sampling based estimators of residual relevant documents exist for ranked review \citep{li2020stop}; our completeness check is a simpler pilot against an independent reference list. Preregistration separates prediction from postdiction \citep{nosek2018preregistration}, templates exist for NLP research and predictive modelling \citep{vanmiltenburg2021preregistering,hofman2023preregistration}, and datasheets document datasets \citep{gebru2021datasheets}. We state which parts of our protocol were committed before they were run and which were not.

\section{Evidence base and method}\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Frame, rows and coding}
We included primary documents from governments, national institutes and standards bodies that were retrieved and read and that contain at least one provision on testing, evaluation, assessment, validation, conformity, red teaming, audit or verification of an AI system before or at deployment. Secondary sources were used only to locate primary ones. Each provision is a row with the clause or page, a paraphrase, the source address, the retrieval date, a binding status and a verification status. Of {{n_rows}} rows, {{n_test_all}} are test provisions (a provision that calls for, or describes as practice, an evaluation that produces evidence), {{n_enabler}} are enablers such as documentation or logging, and {{n_context}} are context such as dates and penalties.

Binding status has five values. \emph{Mandatory} means binding on the stated actor with the duty in operative text. \emph{Conditional} means binding only if a trigger applies, such as a risk class. \emph{Voluntary} is a formal scheme or standard an organisation may adopt. \emph{Guidance} is a recommendation or a description of practice. \emph{Ambiguous} is used where the force of the source cannot be settled from the text. Each test row also has a primary test type from a taxonomy of fifteen types (Appendix~\ref{app:taxonomy}). The headline analysis uses the {{n_test_head}} test rows that are not unverified, unverifiable, superseded or dependent on a portal summary; {{n_test_excl}} test rows are excluded for these reasons, and their effect is bounded in Section~\ref{sec:robust}. Coding is judgemental and was assisted by language model agents (Section~\ref{sec:ethics} and Appendix~\ref{app:llm}).

\subsection{Access rule}
Only standard fetch and search tools were used. If a site rejected a fetch, the attempt was recorded and no workaround was used (no changed user agent, cache, mirror or third party copy). The row is then marked unverified because the source was blocked, excluded from the headline, and the document is listed for a person to download. {{n_blocked}} rows (of all types) were blocked in this way. Two further kinds of row rest on weaker access: {{n_translated}} rows were graded as read through an unofficial or machine translation of the original, and {{n_iso}} rows ({{n_iso_head}} of them headline rows) come from ISO catalogue pages, where the paywalled standard text was not read. Each row records which applies.

\section{What the evidence says}\label{sec:findings}
\paragraph{RQ1: binding provisions are a minority as coded} Of {{n_test_head}} headline test provisions, {{head_mc}} ({{head_mc_pct}}) are mandatory or conditional, {{head_vg}} ({{head_vg_pct}}) are voluntary or guidance and {{head_am}} ({{head_am_pct}}) are ambiguous. Figure~\ref{fig:family} shows that binding text is concentrated: the European Union Act supplies the largest share, followed by Abu Dhabi health regulation, China, the UAE federal instruments, the DIFC and ADGM, and Dubai, while the NIST, Singapore, ISO and general purpose model code families are almost entirely voluntary or guidance. {{rb_fam_any}} of {{rb_fam_n}} families contain any binding test provision, and {{rb_docs_any}} of {{rb_docs}} documents do. These shares describe the provisions that one analyst extracted from documents chosen because they mention testing; how many relevant provisions were never extracted was not measured, and Section~\ref{sec:robust} gives the range under other assumptions.

\paragraph{Required, recommended and described} Among {{nf_n}} headline test rows that are not binding (voluntary, guidance or ambiguous), two independent coders (language model instances working from a written codebook, agreement {{nf_agree_head}}\% on these rows and {{nf_agree}}\% with kappa {{nf_kappa}} on all {{nf_total_coded}} rows coded, including rows outside the headline) separated {{nf_obl}} that use obligation wording inside a non binding document, {{nf_rec}} that recommend a test and {{nf_des}} that only describe practice; the {{nf_dis}} rows on which they disagreed are left as disputed and were not resolved. The distinction matters because a reader who sees ``shall'' in a guidance document may take it for a requirement.

\paragraph{RQ2: binding provisions are no more specific} A common reading is that binding text requires that testing happens and is documented while leaving the metrics and thresholds to the provider. We tested this on all {{sp_n}} headline rows. Two blind coders (language model instances that saw only the framework name, locator and paraphrase, not the binding status) assigned one of four levels: S1 process only, S2 property named, S3 method or metric named, S4 criterion set (Appendix~\ref{app:spec}). The coders agree on {{sp_exact}}\% of rows and within one level on {{sp_within1}}\% (kappa {{sp_kappa}}, 95\% interval {{sp_kappa_ci}}; linear weighted {{sp_kappa_w}}). Figure~\ref{fig:spec} gives the distribution. Binding provisions are more often process only ({{sp_s1_b_A}}\% and {{sp_s1_b_B}}\% by the two coders, against {{sp_s1_n_A}}\% and {{sp_s1_n_B}}\% for voluntary and guidance rows; difference {{sp_s1_d_A}} and {{sp_s1_d_B}} points, document level 95\% intervals {{sp_s1_ci_A}} and {{sp_s1_ci_B}}). Binding rows are therefore less specific at the lowest level and we find no evidence that they are more specific at the higher levels, although the intervals are wide and do not establish equivalence. They are not more likely to name a method, metric or criterion ({{sp_s34_b_A}}\% and {{sp_s34_b_B}}\% against {{sp_s34_n_A}}\% and {{sp_s34_n_B}}\%; intervals {{sp_s34_ci_A}} and {{sp_s34_ci_B}} include zero) or to set a criterion ({{sp_s4_b_A}}\% and {{sp_s4_b_B}}\% against {{sp_s4_n_A}}\% and {{sp_s4_n_B}}\%). The two coders are two instances of the same model working from the same codebook, and they read the analyst's paraphrase, so a shared habit of the model or a consistent way of paraphrasing procedural duties could produce part of the pattern. Binding rows are concentrated in procedural test types such as conformity and risk assessment, so we also compared binding and other rows within the same test type, weighting each type by its number of binding rows ({{sp_adjT_s1_A}} and {{sp_adjT_s1_B}} points for process only, intervals {{sp_adjT_s1_ci_A}} and {{sp_adjT_s1_ci_B}}; {{sp_adjT_s34_A}} and {{sp_adjT_s34_B}} points for naming a method or criterion, intervals {{sp_adjT_s34_ci_A}} and {{sp_adjT_s34_ci_B}}). The process only difference therefore survives holding the test type fixed. Holding the family fixed instead gives {{sp_adjF_s1_A}} and {{sp_adjF_s1_B}} points with intervals {{sp_adjF_s1_ci_A}} and {{sp_adjF_s1_ci_B}} that reach zero, so within a family the evidence is weaker, as it is when each document supplies few rows. These adjusted comparisons were added after the first results were seen. So the simple statement that binding text seldom says how is too strong: about {{sp_s34_b_B}}\% to {{sp_s34_b_A}}\% of binding rows name a method, metric or criterion, but they do so no more often than guidance does, and a larger share of them says only that testing occurs. The coding rests on the analyst's paraphrase, not on the source text, and both coders are models, so this is a measurement of the table and not of the sources.

\paragraph{UAE public sector} We report what the retrieved sources contain and make no claim about what the UAE requires beyond them. One cross sector instrument that uses obligation wording for government entities, the national cyber security policy for AI, concerns AI security. Its scope over emirate level government entities was read differently by two verifiers, and its explicit before deployment wording sits in implementation guidance phrased as should. Other binding UAE testing text in the table is sector or zone specific (Abu Dhabi health, Dubai health, DIFC Regulation 10). We found no UAE federal technical standard for functional, fairness or accuracy testing of AI in government. Several instruments, among them a central bank guidance note, could not be read, and some may not be public. Absence in this table is not evidence of absence in law.

\section{Auditing the evidence base}\label{sec:audit}
\subsection{Baselines (RQ3)}\label{sec:baselines}
We ask how well the two main codings can be predicted by methods much simpler than reading the document, using the {{bl_n}} headline rows. Methods were chosen by the analyst, who had seen the table, and committed with the code: a majority class; a document prior (the most common label among training rows of the same document); a wording rule for binding status (obligation words against recommendation words) and a keyword rule for test type built from the taxonomy definitions; TF-IDF with logistic regression; and a frozen sentence embedding ({{bl_emb_model}}, no fine tuning) with logistic regression whose regularisation is chosen by inner cross validation. The last two are also given a document indicator. Three protocols are used. Row level CV is {{bl_repeats}} times repeated stratified five fold cross validation; it lets rows of one document fall in both training and test folds, so it rewards methods that recognise the document and is reported only to show that effect. Grouped CV uses the same repetitions with every document kept whole in one fold, and leave one family out (LOFO) removes every document of a family from training; these two are the fair protocols. Table~\ref{tab:bl} and Figure~\ref{fig:bl} give accuracy; macro F1 and intervals over documents are in the repository.

Binding status is largely a property of the document. A document prior reaches {{bl_fw5}}\% under row level CV, where other provisions of the same document are in training, against {{bl_maj5}}\% for the majority class, and falls back to the majority rate under grouped CV and LOFO ({{bl_fw5_g}}\% and {{bl_fw5_lofo}}\%) because it has nothing to look up. The same leak inflates the learned models: the embedding with a document indicator drops from {{bl_emb5_fw_cv}}\% under row level CV to {{bl_emb5_fw_g}}\% under grouped CV, and the plain embedding from {{bl_emb5}}\% to {{bl_emb5_g}}\%. Under the fair protocols the learned wording models are not clearly better than the majority class on binding status in five classes (embedding {{bl_emb5_g}}\%, TF-IDF {{bl_tfidf5_g}}\%, against {{bl_maj5_g}}\%; document level interval for the embedding {{bl_emb5_g_ci}}). The wording rule does better ({{bl_wording_g}}\% grouped, interval {{bl_wording_g_ci}}; {{bl_wording3}}\% against {{bl_maj3}}\% in three classes), so the wording of one provision carries some information about its force, but document level intervals overlap heavily and the ordering is suggestive. For test type the picture reverses: the embedding ({{bl_emb_type_g}}\% grouped, {{bl_emb_type_lofo}}\% under LOFO) beats the keyword rule ({{bl_kw}}\%), which beats TF-IDF ({{bl_tfidf_type_g}}\%) and the majority class ({{bl_maj_type_g}}\%); the test type is recoverable from the text of the paraphrase, binding status much less so. These scores show that the coding is regular and not that it is correct, since the labels are the analyst's own and the paraphrase being classified was written by the same analyst. Row level intervals ignore clustering by document ({{bl_fw5_ci_row}} for the document prior under row level CV, against {{bl_fw5_ci_doc}} over documents), so Figure~\ref{fig:bl} and Table~\ref{tab:bl} give intervals over documents where they give any. The keyword lexicon was written by an analyst who had seen the table. Any later classifier must beat the document prior and the wording rule, not only the majority class.

\subsection{Coding reliability (RQ4)}
Two coders who saw the row paraphrase, the document name and the locator, and a written codebook but not the original codes, recoded {{ag_n}} headline rows drawn uniformly at random, without stratification, from the {{n_test_head}} (seed 20261029). Table~\ref{tab:ag} gives percentage agreement and Cohen's kappa for each pair, and Krippendorff's alpha over three coders.

\begin{table}[t]\centering\small

The blind coders agree closely with each other (kappa {{ag_k_blind}} on five classes). Each agrees less with the original coding (kappa {{ag_k_orig_range}}). Of the {{ag_odd_total}} five class disagreements where exactly one coder differs, the original is the odd one out in {{ag_odd_original}} (one further row has three different codes). The original coder used ambiguous on {{ag_amb_orig}} of {{ag_n}} rows, against {{ag_amb_b2}} and {{ag_amb_b3}} for the blind coders, and the commonest pattern is ambiguous in the original against guidance in both blind codings. Two things could produce this and we cannot separate them: the original coder read the whole document while the blind coders read one provision, and the written instructions differed (the blind codebook asks for ambiguous to be used sparingly). The choice of coder moves the headline only a little in this sample: the binding share is {{cs_bind_orig}}\% under the original coding and {{cs_bind_b2}}\% and {{cs_bind_b3}}\% under the blind codings, but the ambiguous share is {{cs_amb_orig}}\% against {{cs_amb_b2}}\% and {{cs_amb_b3}}\%, so the split between non binding and ambiguous depends on the coder. Because the coders saw the analyst's paraphrase and the document name, agreement is probably higher than it would be on raw text; agreement among coders is not accuracy; and all three coders are language models, so errors may be correlated. 

\paragraph{Withholding the document name} The coders above saw the document name, which alone predicts binding status well (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}), so their agreement may overstate how well the codes can be reproduced from a provision alone. We therefore had two further blind coders recode the same {{ag_n}} rows with the document name withheld (they saw the locator and the paraphrase, which can still name the instrument). They agree with each other less closely (kappa {{mk_k_masked}}, {{mk_agree_masked}}\% exact agreement, {{mk_flips_masked}} rows flipping between binding and non binding, against {{mk_flips_named}} for the coders who saw the name). Against the coders who saw the name they reach kappa {{mk_k_vs_named_lo}} to {{mk_k_vs_named_hi}}, and against the original coding {{mk_k_vs_orig_lo}} to {{mk_k_vs_orig_hi}}, which is slightly lower than the named coders reach, a gap within sampling error. \section{The checklist tool (RQ7)}\label{sec:tool}
The tool turns the table into a list of {{n_checklist_items}} checklist items for an organisation. It reads answers to a small set of questions (system type, decision impact, entity zone, audience, data types, acquisition route, European market, hosting) and returns for each item APPLIES, DOES\_NOT\_APPLY or UNKNOWN, with the rows that support the item. Evaluation uses a Kleene three valued logic: a missing or unknown answer never makes a condition true or false. For each item the tool also says which answers would settle it. Whether a source binds the organisation is reported separately and is YES only when the answers settle that the organisation is the actor the source names.

\paragraph{Evaluation} We wrote {{ev_scn}} scenarios from the checklist specification alone, with expected results from an oracle written by the same authors from the same specification, and committed them before the engine was run: {{ev_spec}} with a specified result and {{ev_unspec}} invalid inputs. Ten stated assumptions about under specified cases and five monotone pairs were checked as well, and the expected result of some scenarios depends on those assumptions. The check is therefore of the implementation against its specification and not of the specification. Table~\ref{tab:tool} compares the engine with four naive designs.

\begin{table}[t]\centering\small
{{tex_tool}}
\caption{Item level results over {{ev_spec}} specified scenarios ({{ev_dec}} decisions). Wrongly excluded: said not applicable where applicable or unknown was expected. Wrongly applied: said applicable where not applicable or unknown was expected. Unknown: the engine returned unknown.}\label{tab:tool}
\end{table}

The engine agreed on {{ev_ok}} of {{ev_dec}} decisions ({{ev_acc}}\%) and matched {{ev_exact}} of {{ev_spec}} scenarios exactly, and it never wrongly excluded or wrongly applied an item. The two valued designs score about 57\% because they cannot express unknown, and they are unsafe in opposite ways: the closed design drops items the user cannot yet rule out, and the open design applies items that do not apply. The fairer comparison is with the design that ignores the answers and returns unknown for conditional items ({{ev_struct_acc}}\% with no unsafe errors, but unknown on every conditional item, which is the over cautious behaviour), which the engine beats because it uses the answers; the oracle itself uses three valued logic, so two valued designs are penalised by construction. The scenarios were also used to find and fix two engine defects, so the figure for the final engine is after tuning, which is why the first run is reported. The first run, before any change, with the scenarios committed before the engine was run (commit order is the only timestamp), matched {{ev0_ok}} decisions, wrongly excluded {{ev0_wex}} items (an empty list for a multiple choice question was treated as a known answer) and crashed on {{ev0_crash}} of {{ev_invalid_n}} invalid inputs (a list given for a single value question). These defects were fixed with tests and the scenarios rerun without editing the expectations. Of the {{ev_invalid_n}} invalid inputs, {{ev_invalid_accepted}} were accepted: a comma separated string for a multiple choice question is split into a list by design, which the committed expectations had not anticipated. One scenario still disagrees: an unknown inside a list of values, where the oracle expected APPLIES and the engine conservatively returns UNKNOWN because the specification was ambiguous. A simulation of {{ev_sim_profiles}} random profiles answered one question at a time found {{ev_sim_flips}} flips between APPLIES and DOES\_NOT\_APPLY in {{ev_sim_steps}} steps. The scenarios test that the engine implements the checklist as written; they do not show that the checklist rules are legally correct.

\paragraph{Behaviour on synthetic deployment profiles} To see whether the output differs sensibly between deployments we ran the tool on {{ar_n}} invented profiles (Appendix~\ref{app:arch}). With no answers {{ar_zero_unknown}} of {{ar_items}} items are unknown; with all eight questions answered between {{ar_unk_min}} and {{ar_unk_max}} remain (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). {{ar_vary}} of {{ar_vary_of}} items change state between profiles and {{ar_const_applies}} apply to every profile. \section{Discussion}
\paragraph{What this means for a reader of the sources} As coded, {{head_vg_pct}} of the headline provisions are voluntary or guidance and {{head_am_pct}} cannot be settled from the text, so a reader who treats every provision that mentions testing as a requirement would be wrong about most of them under the coding used here, although the share depends on the assumptions examined in Section~\ref{sec:audit} and the ambiguous share differs between coders. The practical rule that follows is to read the force of the document and the actor before the wording of the clause, since force is a property of the document and the wording of a single clause predicts it only moderately on unseen families (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}).

\paragraph{What this means for authors of requirements} We find no evidence that binding provisions are more specific than guidance, and between a quarter and a third of them ({{sp_s1_b_A}}\% and {{sp_s1_b_B}}\% by the two coders) say only that some testing happens. Where a regime leaves the metric to the provider or to a later standard, a reader cannot tell from the text whether a given test discharges the duty, and the research on benchmark validity suggests that the choice matters \citep{reuel2024betterbench,bean2025measuring}. Requirements that name a method, a data condition or a pass criterion, or that point to a standard that does, would be easier to meet and to audit. We state this as a consequence of the measurement and not as a finding about the intent of any regulator.

\paragraph{What this means for building evidence bases} The audit shows how much a single analyst's table can hide. Binding status was reproduced by blind coders at kappa near 0.65 against the original and near 0.9 between blind coders, or near 0.8 between coders who were not shown the document name, which is compatible with codebook sensitivity at the ambiguous boundary, although random error cannot be excluded; {{vf_err_s}} of {{vf_read}} readable sampled rows had a strict error; and about a fifth ({{vf_unread_pct}}\%) of a fresh sample could not be reread at all. We recommend that comparable tables report agreement against a blind recoding, a fresh verification sample with its unreadable share, and a completeness estimate, and release the coding inputs.

\section{Threats to validity and limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Coding by language model agents.} The original coding, second codings, specificity codings and verifications were produced by agents of the same family, so errors can be correlated. A human second coding and a domain or legal review have not been done. The bias this can introduce is not known in direction.
\item \textbf{Paraphrase circularity.} The blind coders and the baselines read the analyst's paraphrase, not the source text; agreement and predictability are therefore probably overstated as measures of fidelity to the source.
\item \textbf{Unread sources.} {{n_blocked}} rows (of all row types; only test rows would belong to the headline) come from sources that could not be read and are excluded from it, and {{vf_unread_pct}}\% of the fresh verification sample could not be read. Error rates describe readable rows, and the largest unreadable block is the European Union family.
\item \textbf{Completeness and selection.} The completeness pilot found a recall of {{cp_rec}}\% on confirmed candidates, with an unresolved remainder. Documents were selected because they mention testing, so shares of binding provisions describe this sample and not all AI governance. Documents in languages other than English and non public instruments are under represented, and Arabic originals were read for a minority of UAE sources.
\item \textbf{Units of analysis.} A long guidance document yields many rows and a short statute few, so counts are not league tables. Intervals over rows ignore clustering; the document level analyses are wider.
\item \textbf{Currency.} Several documents were being revised at retrieval. Rows carry retrieval dates, and time sensitive items are listed in the repository.
\item \textbf{Model identity.} The exact model name and version of the agents were not recorded during coding and cannot be recovered.
\item \textbf{Extraction recall.} Recall of the extraction step, that is the share of test provisions in a document that became rows, was measured only in the small completeness pilot, not across the full table.
\item \textbf{Omitted and concentrated cases.} Twelve Canadian test rows were entirely blocked and are not in the headline, so a binding Canadian instrument, if any, is missing. Ambiguous rows are concentrated: Saudi documents supply 19 of the {{am_total}} headline ambiguous rows. Conditional provisions, mostly dependent on a risk class, count as binding, so the headline binding share is higher than the mandatory only share ({{mand_pct}}\%, {{mand_n}} rows). No human has coded any row.
\item \textbf{Post hoc analyses.} The grouped cross validation, the masked document coding check, the adjusted specificity comparison and the combined favourable scenario were added after the first results and after review. They were not preregistered, and no adjustment for multiple comparisons was made; intervals should be read as descriptive.
\item \textbf{Tool.} Evaluation is against scenarios and synthetic profiles, with no user study. Applicability rules are the authors' proposals and have not been reviewed by a lawyer. The tool was checked against scenarios written by the same authors and run through the same engine, so agreement with them shows internal consistency and not correctness.
\item \textbf{Literature.} The related work search ran under a restricted budget. Works cited were opened; {{n_full}} of {{n_cited}} were read in full text and the rest at abstract or landing page level, as marked in the repository.
\item \textbf{Not legal advice.} Nothing here states what any law requires of any organisation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Ethics, AI assistance and availability}\label{sec:ethics}
The study uses public documents and involves no human subjects and no personal data. Research subagents built on a large language model searched for and read sources, extracted rows and proposed codes. Separate instances verified rows and made the second codings and the specificity codings. The author set the aims and the rules. The orchestrating agent regraded some verifier outputs against the rubric ({{p3_regraded}} in the third phase and two in the fourth); these calls are recorded in the decision log and a human review of them has not been done. Appendix~\ref{app:llm} lists the reporting items for model assisted work. Verification reduces but does not remove the risk of fabricated or misread content, and the sections above report the measured error rate. Data, code, tests and the decision log are available in the project repository (hudasol/ikhtibar), which carries an all rights reserved notice; reuse requires the authors' permission. Every number computed from the data in this paper is read from a committed result file (counts of read references and regraded rows come from the bibliography file and the decision log), and a test fails if a result file is older than the table it describes.

\section{Conclusion}
In this sample of documents, which were chosen because they mention testing, testing AI before deployment is mentioned far more often than it is required. Fewer than half of headline test provisions are binding under each assumption we tried separately (we did not combine them with sampling uncertainty), we find no evidence that binding provisions are more specific than guidance, and they more often say only that testing happens (the excess holds within test type and less clearly within family), and several test types, among them red teaming, robustness and fairness testing, have few binding provisions. The table is documented and partly independently checked, with a measured error rate and a measured gap in coverage. It is not complete and not error free. The next steps are a human second coding, a domain and legal review, rereading the blocked sources, and a larger completeness benchmark.
